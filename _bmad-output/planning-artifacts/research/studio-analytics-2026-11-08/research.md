# Research: the metrics of an agentic development loop, and how to compare before and after a change

**Decision this research serves:** Epic 40, the analytics section of the studio web, on Tim's request of 2026-11-08: "how long things are taking, how much they are costing me (dollars and tokens)", "what other metrics would be useful to track and also inform settings", "per-story and also over time", and "say I change the model for a certain leg and let it run for a few days I want to be able to see the changes clearly: is it developing faster, slower, or no change? How has the cost been impacted?"

Every `[n]` resolves in the source appendix (§9). Grades: **A** a primary document, specification or paper; **B** a reputable secondary source; **C** a blog, an opinion or a vendor's claim. A claim that rests on a search snippet because the page could not be fetched is marked **(snippet)**. The studio's own records (§2, column "What the studio records") are facts read from the repository and the box, cited by `file:line` at `b9c977af`, not graded.

## 1. Executive summary

**The evidence says: show time and money per story and per stage from the Stage Log and the queue, add the handful of loop-health counts the records already hold (review passes, findings, attempts, halts, guard trips, first-leg green, PR size), judge a change by medians and ranges per band on each side with the per-story points visible and a stated minimum, and leave DORA's production metrics, acceptance rates and the weekly quota out until they have a source.**

Five findings drive that answer:

1. **The industry has converged on a small set, and most of it maps onto what the studio records.** DORA's five keys are lead time, deployment frequency, failed-deployment recovery time, change fail rate and rework rate [1]; SPACE says no single metric measures productivity and asks for at least one perceptual signal beside the counts [5]. The coding-agent products publish per-actor, per-day counts: sessions, lines added and removed, commits and PRs attributed, per-tool accepted and rejected, per-model tokens split into input, output, cache read and cache creation, and an estimated cost in cents [8][9]; Copilot and Cursor count the same things under other names [10][11]. The LLM-ops tools (LangSmith, Langfuse, Helicone, Braintrust) show cost and tokens per trace, latency percentiles and a comparison of two runs against a named baseline [17]–[24]. The studio's Stage Log, queue events, journal and meter already hold the per-stage span, the model and effort, the tokens and the list-price dollars for every landed story since 2026-10-29, and the queue holds the run-level cost and timings since 2026-10-07 (§2).
2. **Dollars are the right currency, at list price, with the cache split visible.** The OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions define no cost attribute at all; they define the token split (`gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `output_tokens`, `cache_read.input_tokens`, `cache_write.input_tokens`) and, for Anthropic, say `input_tokens` must be the sum of uncached, cache-read and cache-write tokens because Anthropic's own `input_tokens` field excludes the cached ones [12][13][14]. Claude Code computes its `/usage` dollars locally at list price [25]; its OpenTelemetry metrics carry `claude_code.cost.usage` in USD and `claude_code.token.usage` with the type `input`, `output`, `cacheRead` or `cacheCreation`, and since 2.1.300 the 5m and 1h cache-creation split [27]. The studio's `meter.py` already prices that way (`meter.py:444-541`), and its `prices.json` matches the pricing page read on 2026-11-07 for every model it names [30]; it lacks Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5, which the page now prices, so a story routed to either would be a named refusal today.
3. **The weekly quota has no supported source.** The Max plan's binding constraint is the weekly usage, not dollars (`kb/execution-pipeline-model-routing.md:70-73`). Claude Code shows plan usage in the terminal (`/usage`) and exports per-request cost and tokens over OpenTelemetry on every plan, but the Admin Usage and Cost APIs cover API-key usage only, never Pro, Max or Team sign-ins [28][29][31]. The quota share is therefore an open question for the owner (addendum §5 Q1), with an OpenTelemetry exporter on the box as the one route that would give per-request figures live.
4. **With three to ten stories a side, the honest comparison is points, medians and ranges, never a mean or a p-value.** Durations are right-skewed, so the median and a tail percentile summarise them and the mean misleads [37][38]. The exact Mann-Whitney test cannot reach p 0.05 below four per side (the smallest two-sided p at 3 against 3 is 0.10, derived from the null distribution); bootstrap intervals under-cover at small n [34][35]; Wheeler's process-behaviour charts call limits computed from fewer than ten points "soft" and the NHS practice labels them "trial" below twelve and recomputes them per point [39][40][41]. Every product that offers a before/after draws the change as a vertical mark on the time series and compares against a named baseline [43]–[46], and the known traps are regression to the mean (a change made after a bad run looks like an improvement), Simpson's paradox when the two sides differ in story size, and perception (METR's developers believed they were 20 % faster while measured 19 % slower) [47][48][6].
5. **The comparison must be per band and per route, and the route is in the rows.** Each Stage Log row carries the model and effort its stage ran at (`plansync.py:2198-2231`), so "before and after the change" can be read as "stories whose dev row ran on route A against route B" as well as by date, which removes the one confound a date cut cannot: a story queued before the change but run after it. The band (S, M, L) is the other confound and the comparison stratifies on it (§7).

**Biggest caveat.** The studio's history is short and uneven: 197 Stage Log rows over 23 stories, 79 of them priced, nothing per stage before Story 35.8 (2026-10-29), the meter's per-leg usage (the only place the cache share is split) in the sandbox's ledgers rather than the host's, and the engine journal gone with the worktree at every close-out (§2.5). The first weeks of the analytics will show "not enough stories yet" more often than a verdict; the epic's value is that from now on every story lands into a store, and that a verdict, when it comes, is one the owner can trust.

### 2.4 Not taken, with the reason

- **Acceptance rate** (accepted over accepted plus rejected per tool) [8][10][11]: a human-in-the-loop measure; the studio's loop has no human accepting edits. The nearest equivalent is the gate's findings and the first-attempt landing, which are shown.
- **DORA's failed-deployment recovery time and rework rate** [1]: there is no production. The first-leg red share and the hand-fixed Windows classes are the studio's instability signals.
- **Lines of code attributed** [8][9]: every line here is the loop's; PR size is shown as a sizing check, not as output.
- **The perceptual SPACE dimension** [5]: one owner; his check-ins and notes (the owner's annotation route) are the perceptual record.
- **Automatic tuning** from the figures: the owner changes a knob; the page shows what happened (addendum §6).

### 2.5 What the records cannot give today

- Nothing per stage before Story 35.8 (2026-10-29). Epics 29 to 34 have run-level cost and timings in the queue (2026-10-07 onward) and the handoffs' figures; Epics 20 to 24 have the trial record's hand-built table [kb] and twelve legacy meter observations whose figures sit in a description string in the committed ledger (`measurements/tim-Tim-PC-2.jsonl`, the lines reading `meter: story=… usd=… wall_clock_s=… attempts=…`).
- The engine journal and the transcript copies live in the run's worktree and its `.bmad-loop/archive`, which the close-out removes (`ended.closeout.worktree: "removed"`; `closeout.ts:77-85`). What survives is what the hook wrote into the Stage Log and what the meter wrote into the ledger. A figure the store wants from the journal (attempts, passes, halts, guard trips) is readable for a live run and must be captured at the run's end from then on; for past runs it comes from the Stage Log's rows (one row per pass, the outcome column) and the queue's events.
- The cache share is split in the meter's `legs.<leg>.usage` and `state.json`, neither of which reached the host ledger for the Stage Log era (the `kind: meter` observations are absent from the host file; the twelve legacy ones predate the structured fields). Until the harvest lands them, the store shows `tokens_in` "including cache reads" and the split as unknown.
- The Stage Log is append-only and its columns are fixed; a derived figure never becomes a column. The store derives, the log stays the record.

## 3. What the industry measures (delivery and coding agents)

- DORA's metric set is five: change lead time, deployment frequency, failed-deployment recovery time (replacing "time to restore"), change fail rate and deployment rework rate. **A** [1].
- The 2024 DORA report associated a 25 % increase in AI adoption with an estimated 1.5 % decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2 % decrease in stability, beside gains in documentation quality (7.5 %), code quality (3.4 %) and review speed (3.1 %); 39 % of respondents reported little or no trust in AI-generated code. **A** [2][3].
- The 2025 report ("State of AI-assisted Software Development") puts adoption at 90 %, a median of two hours a day with AI, and reverses the throughput sign (AI adoption now associated with higher throughput) while "software delivery instability continues to rise"; AI is framed as an amplifier of what an organisation already is. **A** for adoption and the reversal; **B** for the instability direction (the full PDF exceeded the fetch; InfoQ's reading) [4].
- SPACE's five dimensions are satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, efficiency and flow; "productivity cannot be measured by a single metric or dimension". **A** for the dimensions; the "at least three dimensions, one perceptual" guidance is **B/C** (secondary summaries) [5].
- METR's July 2025 randomised trial: 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 issues, AI-allowed tasks took 19 % longer (CI +2 % to +39 %) while the developers forecast a 24 % speed-up and still believed in a 20 % one afterwards; the February 2026 update over 57 developers and 800 tasks measured returning developers 18 % faster (CI −38 % to +9 %) and new ones 4 % faster (CI −15 % to +9 %), which METR calls a downward-biased lower bound. **A** [6]. The lesson for the studio is in §7: perception is not a measurement, and the confidence intervals at these sizes are wide.
- A study of 567 Claude Code PRs across 157 open-source projects: 83.8 % eventually merged; of the merged, 54.9 % merged unmodified and 45.1 % needed changes. **A** [6a].
- The published coding-agent metric sets: Claude Code's Analytics API answers per actor per day `num_sessions`, lines added and removed, commits and PRs by Claude Code, per-tool accepted and rejected, per-model tokens (input, output, cache read, cache creation) and `estimated_cost` in cents **A** [8]; its dashboards show lines accepted, suggestion accept rate, active users, spend and PR attribution with a 21-day-before/2-day-after window **A** [9]; GitHub Copilot's usage metrics (preview, 1-year retention) count active users, interactions, code generation and acceptance activity, lines suggested, added and deleted, agent, chat and CLI use, and PR review times **A** [10]; Cursor's team analytics count tab completions accepted, agent lines accepted and the share of committed code from AI **A** for the dashboard, **C** for the API field names [11].
- A practitioner set for agents: autonomy rate (PRs shipped without a human rewrite), cost per merged PR, human intervention rate, defect or rollback rate, cycle time, each against a pre-agent baseline. **C** [7]. No published "tokens per accepted change" standard was found.
- SWE-bench's submission checklist has no cost field; the leaderboard draws cost charts and Princeton's HAL publishes per-run dollars (the page returned 404 on the day). **A** for the checklist; **B/C** for the charts (snippet) [16].

## 4. LLM-ops observability and Claude Code's own surfaces

- **The OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions** (stability: Development; now in the `semantic-conventions-genai` repository): an inference span named `{gen_ai.operation.name} {gen_ai.request.model}`, kind CLIENT, with `gen_ai.operation.name` and `gen_ai.provider.name` required and `gen_ai.request.model`, `gen_ai.response.model`, `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.output_tokens`, `gen_ai.response.finish_reasons` recommended **A** [12]; the cache attributes `gen_ai.usage.cache_read.input_tokens` and (current) `gen_ai.usage.cache_write.input_tokens` (the archived registry says `cache_creation`), both included in `input_tokens` **A** [13]; for Anthropic, `gen_ai.provider.name` is `anthropic` and `input_tokens` is the sum of uncached, cache-read and cache-write tokens **A** [14]; agent spans `create_agent`, `invoke_agent`, `execute_tool` with `gen_ai.agent.*`, `gen_ai.conversation.id`, `gen_ai.tool.*` **A** [12]; metrics `gen_ai.client.token.usage` (histogram by `gen_ai.token.type`), `gen_ai.client.operation.duration`, and the newer per-type counters **A** [15]. **No cost attribute exists in the conventions.** **A** [13]. The studio's `meter._tokens` already uses the same four buckets (`meter.py:397-420`); if the store ever exports, these are the names.
- **LangSmith**: prebuilt dashboard sections for traces (count, latency, error rate), LLM calls, cost and tokens by token type, tools and run types, feedback scores; p50 and p99 only in custom charts; before/after as experiments against a source (baseline) experiment with per-key regressions and improvements counted **A** [17][18].
- **Langfuse**: traces over time, model cost, latency percentiles, score analytics, model usage; prompt A/B by labels with a per-version metrics tab (median latency, tokens, cost, count, scores); dataset experiments against a designated baseline with score, cost and latency deltas; the advice to record the app commit and the prompt or model version in the run metadata **A** [19][20][21].

- **Braintrust**: experiments compared against a baseline (explicit, project default, or the latest on the same git branch), rows aligned by input, a "comparison grade" of improvement, regression, trade-off or tie from latency, cost and errors, token metrics and estimated cost beside the scores **A** [23][24].
- **Claude Code's cost surfaces**: `/usage` (with `/cost` and `/stats` as aliases) shows total cost, API and wall duration, code changes, usage by model (input, output, cache read, cache write, dollars), a prompt-cache line (requests, the share of input from cache, misses, warm and cold) since 2.1.251, and for plan users an attribution breakdown by skill, sub-agent, plugin and MCP server with 24-hour and 7-day toggles; the dollars are computed locally at list price; totals reset on `/clear` **A** [25]. Anthropic's enterprise averages: about 13 USD per developer per active day, 150 to 250 USD a month **A** [26].
- **Claude Code's OpenTelemetry export** (every plan; `CLAUDE_CODE_ENABLE_TELEMETRY=1`, `OTEL_METRICS_EXPORTER`, `OTEL_LOGS_EXPORTER`): metrics `claude_code.session.count`, `lines_of_code.count`, `pull_request.count`, `commit.count`, `cost.usage` (USD, with `model`, `effort`, `speed`, `agent.name`, `skill.name`, `plugin.name`, `mcp_server.name`), `token.usage` (`type` input, output, cacheRead, cacheCreation), `code_edit_tool.decision`, `active_time.total`; events `claude_code.api_request` (`model`, `duration_ms`, `cost_usd`, the four token counts, the 5m/1h cache-creation split since 2.1.300, `effort`, `speed`), `tool_result`, `tool_decision`, `api_error` **A** [27]. This is the one documented stream that would give the studio per-request cost, duration and the cache split live from the sandbox's sessions, on the Max plan; it needs a collector on the box and is the plan's option (b) for the owner's Q1.
- **The Admin APIs**: the Usage and Cost API (`/v1/organizations/usage_report/messages`, `/cost_report`) and the newer Usage and Cost Analytics API (`/v1/organizations/analytics/usage` and `/cost`, grouping by model, actor, service tier, context window and more) cover API-key usage; the Claude Code Analytics API states it covers only API-authenticated usage, not Pro, Max or Team sign-ins **A** [28][29][31].
- **List prices, read 2026-11-07** (USD per MTok: input, 5m cache write, 1h cache write, cache read, output): Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 10 / 12.50 / 20 / 0.25 / 50; Opus 5.5 4 / 5 / 8 / 0.20 / 20; Opus 5 5 / 6.25 / 10 / 0.50 / 25; Sonnet 5.5 2 / 2.50 / 4 / 0.10 / 10; Sonnet 5 2 / 2.50 / 4 / 0.20 / 10; Haiku 5.5 (prompts to 100 K) 0.10 / 0.125 / 0.20 / 0.01 / 0.50; Haiku 4.5 1 / 1.25 / 2 / 0.10 / 5. The multipliers: a 5m write is 1.25 × input, a 1h write 2 ×, a cache read 0.1 × except Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 (0.05 ×) and Fable and Mythos 5.1 (0.025 ×); batch is half price; `inference_geo: us` is 1.1 × on the 4.6+ models; the 4.7+ tokenizer yields about 30 % more tokens for the same text **A** [30]. Against the studio's `prices.json` (dated 2026-09-28): every named model matches; Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 are missing (a routed story on either is a named refusal, `meter.py:1-60`); the tokenizer note means a token count is not comparable across model generations, which is one more reason to judge on dollars.

## 5. CI insights (what a build dashboard shows by default)

- **GitHub Actions** metrics (Insights, organisation and repository): usage (minutes, run volume, by workflow, job, repository, OS and runner) and performance (average run time, average queue time, job failures and failure rate), over the current week, month, 30, 90 days, a year or a custom range up to 100 days; UTC days; skipped runs excluded; CSV export. Averages only; no p50 or p90 is documented **A** [32].
- **CircleCI Insights**: per workflow the status of the last 100 runs, the success rate, the duration p50 against p95 and the credits; per job the credits, the p95, the runs and the success rate; a branch filter; up to 24 hours of lag; test insights (flaky, most failed, slowest) on a separate page **A**; the glossary's MTTR (the time from a failed run to the next green one) and the p95 benchmark **B** (snippet) [33].
- **Buildkite**: cluster insights (queue wait, jobs waiting, agent utilisation, pass rate, over 1 hour, 24 hours or 7 days); the best-practice page says to track build duration, throughput, success and failure rate by pipeline and branch, queue wait and flaky-test retries **A** [34a].
- The studio's equivalents: the host leg's `duration_ms` and `suite_durations` (duration and its breakdown), `green` and the legs history (success rate, MTTR between a red leg and the re-run's green), `enqueued` to `claimed` (queue wait). The medians and ranges the plan shows are the p50 and the spread CircleCI draws; p95 is not meaningful at the studio's counts and is replaced by the range.

## 7. The comparison method: "I changed the model for a leg on day X"

The question has three honest answers (faster, slower, no clear change) and one more that the page must be willing to give: not enough stories yet. The method:

1. **Define the two sides by the route, not only by the date.** A "change" is an annotation (`settings-changed`, a routing commit, an owner's note). The before side is the landed stories whose relevant stage row ran on the old route; the after side those on the new route (the Stage Log row carries the model and effort, `plansync.py:2198-2231`). A story queued before the change and run after it falls on the right side by its row, which a date cut would misplace. The date cut is still drawn (the hairline on the charts) because that is how the owner remembers it.
2. **Stratify by band.** S, M and L stories differ by a factor of three in the band estimates and more in practice (Epic 36: 8.68 to 38.96 USD). Pooling them invites Simpson's paradox: if the after side happens to hold smaller stories, the pooled median falls for no reason that the model caused [48]. The compare view shows one row per band and a pooled row only when both sides have the same band mix, which it checks and says.
3. **Summarise each side by its median and its range** (the minimum, the maximum and the interquartile range), with every point drawn. Durations and costs are right-skewed; a mean of five stories is moved by one slow one [37][38]. No mean appears on the page.
4. **The minimum sample and the verdict rule.** With fewer than three landed stories on a side, the page says "not enough stories yet (n of 3)" and shows the points without a verdict. With three or more on each side: "faster" (or "slower", "cheaper", "dearer") when the medians differ by more than a fifth and the interquartile ranges do not overlap; otherwise "no clear change". The rule is deliberately a fifth and an overlap test rather than a p-value: the exact Mann-Whitney test's smallest two-sided p is 0.10 at 3 against 3, 0.029 at 4 against 4 and 0.008 at 5 against 5 (derived from the exact null distribution; the test is documented at [34]), so a significance claim below four a side is arithmetically impossible and above it is rarely informative at these sizes; bootstrap percentile intervals under-cover in small samples [35]; the practitioner thresholds (exact tests below about seven a side, bootstrap medians provisional below fifteen) are **C** [36]. The verdict text always carries both counts, both medians and both ranges, so the owner reads the evidence, not only the word. The constants (three, a fifth) are one line each in the supervisor and are the owner's to move (addendum §4 choice 6).
5. **Draw the baseline's natural variation.** For the over-time view, the rolling median of the last five is the trend; a process-behaviour (XmR) view is offered on the stage charts once a side has twelve points, with the limits labelled "trial" below that and recomputed per point, after Wheeler's "soft below ten degrees of freedom" and the NHS practice of locking at twelve [39][40][41]. A shift is several consecutive points on one side of the old median, which the chart says in words; it says when, never why [42].
6. **Name the confounds on the page.** Beside every verdict: the band mix, the number of review passes on each side (a pass costs a round), the cache share where known (cache warmth moves cost without moving quality), and any other annotation that fell between the two sides' dates (a second change, a supervisor restart, a prices.json date). Regression to the mean is called out when the change was made within two stories of a run that tripped a guard or went red [47].
7. **Perception is not a measurement.** The owner's notes are annotations, never data points; METR's developers were sure of a speed-up the clock did not show [6].

What the products do, for the record: Grafana draws point and region annotations with hover text from any datasource [43]; Datadog overlays events and change tracking (deployments, flags, config) and offers "compare time" [44]; Amplitude and Mixpanel pin tagged annotations as vertical markers with a side list [45][46]; the LLM-ops tools compare against a named baseline experiment or prompt version [18][21][23]. The studio's compare view is the baseline comparison with the annotation as its anchor.
